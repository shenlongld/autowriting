# The title frames Qinghai's ecological progress through green water, green power, and green computing.
Title: From Green Water to Green Power to Green Computing: Qinghai's Practice in China's Ecological Progress

# The opening greeting establishes a formal speech setting.
Distinguished guests, ladies and gentlemen, friends,

# The opening hook raises the civilizational importance of protecting nature while pursuing development.
At the headwaters of great rivers, civilization is reminded of an ancient truth: the future of humankind is written not only in the rise of cities, the expansion of industry, or the acceleration of technology, but also in the clarity of water, the vitality of grasslands, the stability of climate, and the wisdom with which development is governed. A nation may build roads, factories, networks, and markets; yet if it fails to protect the natural foundations of life, its prosperity will be fragile, its progress incomplete, and its promise to the next generation unfulfilled.

# This paragraph defines the presentation's central question about sustainable development through Qinghai.
This presentation is an introduction to China's ecological progress through one powerful case: Qinghai. Our focus is not a slogan, but a transformation. Our question is not whether development matters, for development is essential to human dignity. Our question is how development can be pursued without exhausting the land that sustains it, without polluting the waters that nourish it, and without narrowing the future that belongs to those who come after us.

# This paragraph introduces Qinghai's strategic ecological position as China's Water Tower.
Qinghai offers a vivid answer. Located on the northeastern part of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, it is the birthplace of the Yangtze River, the Yellow River, and the Lancang River. It is often called China's "Water Tower," a name that is not poetic decoration but strategic reality. From this highland, source water flows across mountains and plains, supporting ecosystems, agriculture, industries, and communities far downstream. To understand Qinghai is to understand why ecological protection in one region can become ecological security for a nation; why a local choice can become a national contribution; why the fate of rivers, energy, and technology can converge on the same plateau.

# This paragraph presents the ecological issue and explains why protection became central to modernization.
The issue before Qinghai was once severe and unmistakable. Some river source areas faced shrinking lakes, degraded grasslands, ecological fragility, and the pressure of unsustainable extraction. The reason this mattered went far beyond provincial boundaries: when the headwaters are weakened, downstream security is weakened; when grasslands retreat, biodiversity and climate resilience retreat with them; when development consumes its own ecological base, short-term growth becomes long-term liability. The conclusion China drew was firm: ecological protection had to be placed at the center of modernization, not at its margins.

# This paragraph summarizes the shift from natural resource advantages to green development contributions.
That is why Qinghai's story can be summarized by a remarkable movement from "three natural strengths" to "three green contributions." In the past, people spoke of good water, good sunlight, and good wind. Today, Qinghai is increasingly known for green water, green power, and green computing. This is not merely a change in vocabulary. It is a change in development logic. Good water, sunlight, and wind describe what nature gives; green water, power, and computing describe what responsible governance, technological innovation, and national strategy can create.

# This paragraph details green water achievements in Sanjiangyuan and river source protection.
Green water gives the clearest starting point. Since the launch of major protection and restoration efforts in Sanjiangyuan, and since China's first national park system pilot began there in 2016, Qinghai has advanced systematic conservation of mountains, rivers, forests, farmland, lakes, grasslands, and deserts. The province has strengthened biodiversity protection, improved water conservation, and built a multi-level river and lake governance system. Today, the Sanjiangyuan region conserves about 40.895 billion cubic meters of water annually and sends roughly 60 to 90 billion cubic meters of source water to downstream areas each year. The main streams of the Yellow River, the Yangtze River, and the Lancang River in Qinghai maintain water quality at Grade II or above. These figures are not abstract. They mean safer water systems, healthier ecosystems, and a stronger ecological foundation for hundreds of millions of people.

# This paragraph explains how institutions and local participation make ecological protection sustainable.
Behind these achievements stands an institutional answer to a practical problem. Protection cannot rely on sentiment alone. It needs responsibilities that are clear, mechanisms that are durable, and people who can participate. Qinghai has established a river and lake chief system from the provincial level down to the village level. More than 3,500 rivers and over 500 lakes now have dedicated guardians. In Sanjiangyuan National Park, 17,211 herders serve as certified ecological conservation workers, and across Qinghai there are more than 140,000 ecological public-service positions. What does this prove? It proves that environmental governance becomes stronger when the people are not treated as bystanders, but as stewards; not as a cost of conservation, but as the very force that makes conservation sustainable.

# This paragraph introduces Qinghai's renewable energy potential and the challenge of converting resources into clean power.
A second transformation is unfolding through green power. Qinghai's natural endowment is extraordinary: abundant water resources, vast desert land, strong wind, and intense sunlight. Available land suitable for photovoltaic development reaches about 100,000 square kilometers, accounting for roughly 12 percent of China's comparable resources. The exploitable solar energy potential exceeds 3 billion kilowatts, and exploitable wind energy is more than 75 million kilowatts, with many areas enjoying annual average wind speeds of 6 to 8 meters per second. The challenge, however, was never the existence of resources. The challenge was whether resources could be converted into clean energy at scale, linked to national demand, and integrated with ecological improvement rather than ecological damage.

# This paragraph uses Talatan to show how clean energy development can restore land and support livelihoods.
Qinghai's answer has been decisive. By October 2025, its clean energy installed capacity had exceeded 73 million kilowatts, accounting for more than 93 percent of the province's total power capacity. The Talatan area in Gonghe County, once a barren stretch of wind and sand, has become a vast photovoltaic landscape covering about 350 square kilometers. Solar panels now form a blue ocean across the plateau. The panels reduce wind speed, slow soil moisture evaporation, and allow grass to return beneath them. Sheep graze under the panels while electricity is generated above them. In this single image, one can see the meaning of high-quality development: energy is produced, land is restored, livelihoods are supported, and carbon intensity is reduced.

# This paragraph explains how Qinghai's green power serves national energy transition through transmission projects.
Green power has also become a bridge between regions. The Qinghai-Henan +/-800 kilovolt ultra-high-voltage direct-current project, placed into operation in 2020, stretches 1,587 kilometers from Hainan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture in Qinghai to Zhumadian in Henan. As a key project in China's west-to-east power transmission strategy, it has sent more than 80 billion kilowatt-hours of green electricity from Qinghai over five years. More broadly, Qinghai's green power now reaches 16 provincial-level regions, and since the beginning of the 14th Five-Year Plan period, cumulative outbound green electricity has exceeded 110 billion kilowatt-hours. In 2024 alone, clean energy investment in the province reached 40.6 billion yuan. The conclusion is clear: Qinghai is not only protecting a water source; it is becoming a national clean-energy highland.

# This paragraph introduces green computing as the digital-age extension of Qinghai's ecological progress.
The next frontier is green computing, where ecological progress enters the commanding heights of the digital age. The demand is increasingly urgent. Artificial intelligence, cloud services, industrial internet, and scientific computing all require enormous amounts of electricity. If that electricity is carbon-intensive, digital development will carry a heavy environmental burden. Qinghai's advantage lies in linking low-carbon power with high-value computing. In this way, the province turns green electricity into green computing capacity and transforms a resource advantage into a strategic industrial advantage.

# This paragraph provides data on Qinghai's green computing infrastructure and electricity-computing coordination.
Concrete progress is already visible. The China Unicom Sanjiangyuan National Big Data Base in Xining has attracted investment of about 2.6 billion yuan. Its computing capacity has reached around 9,000 petaflops and is expected to rise to about 13,000 petaflops. Qinghai's clean energy and green computing dispatch center, launched in June 2024, monitors power data from 559 clean energy stations and carbon-emission curves from 912 energy-consuming enterprises. It has integrated data from major telecom operators and Qinghai University, forming a provincial mechanism for coordinated electricity and computing dispatch. By September, Qinghai had built about 190,000 standard racks, and its computing scale had grown nearly fortyfold within a year. In the language of the new era, Qinghai is turning watts into bits, green electricity into digital intelligence, and ecological advantage into new productive forces.

# This paragraph answers what tangible changes China's environmental initiatives have brought.
Taken together, these developments answer the first question raised by today's topic: what tangible changes have China's new environmental initiatives brought about? They have brought clearer water at the river sources, stronger protection across national parks, more stable employment for ecological guardians, cleaner electricity across long-distance transmission lines, restored grasslands under solar panels, and new green industries in data centers and computing clusters. They have changed landscapes, changed livelihoods, changed energy structures, and changed the way a region understands its place in the national economy.

# This paragraph frames the harder question of balancing development needs with long-term sustainability.
A harder question follows: how has China balanced short-term development with long-term sustainability? Qinghai's experience suggests that the balance is not achieved by choosing one side and abandoning the other. It is achieved by redefining development itself. Short-term gains that damage ecological foundations are not true gains. Industrial projects that ignore environmental limits are not true progress. Growth that leaves behind degraded rivers, exhausted grasslands, and unpaid ecological debts is not worthy of the name modernization.

# This paragraph explains Qinghai's balance between ecological boundaries and green economic pathways.
Therefore, Qinghai has drawn boundaries as well as opened pathways. It protects what must be protected through national parks, ecological red lines, river and lake governance, and public stewardship. It develops what can be developed through clean energy, green industry, eco-tourism, organic agriculture and animal husbandry, and computing powered by renewable electricity. It refuses the false choice between ecology and economy by insisting that ecological value can become economic strength when institutions, technology, and people move together.

# This paragraph uses the Muli coalfield as a lesson in abandoning unsustainable development.
There is a sober lesson in the transformation of the Muli coalfield. Coal resources once brought fiscal benefits, but disorderly exploitation damaged the fragile ecology of the southern Qilian Mountains. In 2020, local mining enterprises were shut down, and restoration became a priority. Today, areas such as Tianjun are turning toward ecological animal husbandry and tourism; in 2024, tourism revenue there reached 212 million yuan. This shift does not deny the importance of development. It clarifies the standard of development. Development must be lawful, responsible, forward-looking, and worthy of the land on which it stands.

# This paragraph presents Qinghai as a window into China's broader ecological progress.
Qinghai's practice is not presented today as an isolated miracle. It is presented as a window into China's broader ecological progress. It shows how policy can become practice, how practice can become measurable change, and how measurable change can become a path for high-quality development. The lesson is profound: ecological civilization is not built by declarations alone. It is built by rivers guarded day after day, by grasslands restored season after season, by power lines carrying clean energy across mountains and plains, by data centers running on green electricity, and by ordinary people who find dignity and income in protecting the land they call home.

# This paragraph restates the issue-reason-conclusion logic of the whole introduction.
As we begin this presentation, I invite all of us to view Qinghai not merely as a place on a map, but as a living answer to the questions before us. What has changed? Water quality, energy structure, industrial direction, community participation, and regional responsibility have changed. Why has it changed? Because China has placed ecological protection within the architecture of modernization and has sought to turn environmental responsibility into development momentum. What should we conclude? That the future of development will belong not to those who consume nature fastest, but to those who can align prosperity with restraint, innovation with responsibility, and national progress with the health of the planet.

# The closing call invites the audience to examine Qinghai's practice and reflect on shared responsibility.
Let this be the starting point of our discussion: from green water to green power to green computing, Qinghai demonstrates that ecological progress can be seen, measured, shared, and carried forward. Let us look carefully at this practice, think deeply about its meaning, and carry into our own choices the responsibility it reveals. For in the end, the rivers we protect, the energy we choose, and the technologies we build will decide not only the quality of development, but the quality of the future itself.
